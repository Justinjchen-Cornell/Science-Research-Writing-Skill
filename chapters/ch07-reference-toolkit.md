# Chapter 7: The Reference Toolkit — Appendices A-D & Title Mechanics

## Core Idea
The appendices are the book's permanent reference stack: abbreviations, prefixes, Latin/Greek plurals, and a 200+ verb bank for writing about research. Keep them beside you while drafting — they solve most "how do I say this professionally" moments without re-reading the book.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Latin/Greek plurals (Appendix C)**:
  - -um → -a: datum/data, medium/media, stratum/strata
  - -us → -i: nucleus/nuclei, stimulus/stimuli, radius/radii
  - -is → -es: analysis/analyses, basis/bases, hypothesis/hypotheses, thesis/theses, crisis/crises
  - -on → -a: criterion/criteria, phenomenon/phenomena
  - -ix/-ex → -ices: matrix/matrices, index/indices, apex/apices
  - -a → -ae: formula/formulae, larva/larvae
  - Watch the traps: apparatus, species, series (same in singular/plural); corpus → corpora
- **Abbreviations (Appendix A)**: c. (circa, approximately), cf. (confer, compare), et al. (and others), vs. (as opposed to), i.e. (that is / in other words), e.g. (for example), N.B. (note well), p.a. (per year)
- **Prefixes (Appendix B)** — the 56-prefix system, grouped by meaning; key clusters:
  - Not/opposite: a-, an-, non-, un-, in-, im-, ir-, dis-, mal-, ill-, mis-
  - Against: anti-, contra-, counter-
  - With/together: co-, con-; same: homo-, iso-, equi-; different: hetero-
  - Number: uni-/mono- (1), di-/bi- (2), poly-/multi- (many), cent- (hundred), kilo- (thousand), milli- (thousandth), mega- (million), semi- (half)
  - Position: pre-/fore-/ante- (before), post- (after), inter- (between), circum- (around), sub-/hypo-/infra- (under), super-/hyper- (above), tele- (far), para- (beside)
  - Change/time: re- (again), retro- (backwards), meta- (change), chron- (time)
- **Useful Verbs Bank (Appendix D)**: 200+ verbs for every rhetorical move — establish/confirm/validate, challenge/refute/disprove, measure/quantify/estimate, derive/calculate/compute, propose/suggest/argue, investigate/examine/explore, reveal/indicate/imply, improve/enhance/optimise, compare/contrast/correlate. Use specific verbs instead of did/showed/found; recycle them in the Introduction's "present paper" sentences

## Key Concepts
- **Prefix meanings multiply word knowledge**: learning 56 prefixes × 3-4 example words decodes hundreds of scientific terms
- **Determiner agreement**: "data" (plural) takes plural verbs — a common error even among native speakers; "phenomena" is plural (phenomenon = singular)
- **Appendix D verbs map to the models**: they slot directly into the vocabulary lists of Units 1-5

## Mental Models
- "The reference toolkit converts knowledge into production speed" — look up, don't guess; the appendices exist to be consulted
- "Specific verbs = specific meanings": extract/optimise/precipitate tell the reader exactly what was 'done'; do/show/find tell them nothing

## Anti-patterns
- **Incorrect Latin plurals in your own writing**: write "the data show" not "the data shows"; "these phenomena" not "this phenomena"
- **i.e. vs e.g. confusion**: i.e. = that is (restatement); e.g. = for example (example)
- **Abbreviation misuse in formal text**: don't use "&" or informal abbreviations; use the Latin set above only

## Worked Example
Prefix decoding in action: a title like "Semi-continuous nanofiltration-coupled Heck reactions" → semi- (half/partially) + continuous (uninterrupted) = partially-intermittent processing; coupled = linked (co-/con- family). Reading the prefix system lets you parse technical titles without a dictionary.

## Key Takeaways
1. Keep Appendix D's verb bank open while drafting — replace generic verbs with specific ones
2. Master the Latin plural patterns (-us→-i, -is→-es, -um→-a, -on→-a, -a→-ae) — they cover 90% of science terms
3. Use prefixes to decode unfamiliar technical vocabulary
4. i.e. = restatement, e.g. = example, cf. = compare, et al. = and others
5. Check Guide for Authors for abbreviation conventions before submitting
