# Chapter 1: The Core Method — How to Use This Book

## Core Idea
The book's entire strategy in one line: **carefully examine good examples of the writing you want to produce → identify and master their structure, grammar and vocabulary → apply them in your own writing.** Science writing is conventional, so a small set of models + vocabulary covers almost everything.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Examine→Identify→Apply Cycle**: 
  - When to use: every time you write a section of a paper or thesis
  - How: (1) collect 3-4 real research papers from your field ("target articles"); (2) mark what each sentence *does* (not what it says); (3) build a transferable model; (4) write using the model + its vocabulary list
- **The Three Questions** (asked for every section):
  - How do writers normally *start* this section?
  - What type of information goes in it, and in what order?
  - How do writers normally *end* it?
- **The Symmetrical Shape**: a research article narrows (general→specific) in the Introduction and widens (specific→general) in the Discussion/Conclusion — the Discussion revisits Introduction elements in reverse order
- **The Research Article Shape**: Title → Abstract → Introduction → [Methodology + Results = central report section] → Discussion/Conclusion → Acknowledgements → References
- **Target Article Selection Rules**: from English-speaking institutions; <15 A4 pages; topic close to yours; clearly subtitled IMRD sections. Never use book chapters as targets (different structure)
- **Writing Order Advice**: write the central report sections first, the Introduction after you know what you did/found; the Abstract last (it derives from the article)

## Key Concepts
- **Conventional model**: a familiar structure readers can parse instantly; language errors matter less when the organization is conventional
- **Conventional language**: the limited set of non-technical "code" words used in science writing (attempt, conduct, interpret, evaluate, determine, implement, formulate, classify, correlate, enhance)
- **Content words vs function**: a model is only transferable if it describes what sentences *do*, not topic-specific words (polymer, traffic)

## Mental Models
- "Show readers the wall before you examine the bricks" — general picture first, details second
- "The helicopter ride over Paris" — skim before you read (like reading the last page of a murder mystery: you read faster when you know where things are)
- "Write for the reader who is learning from you" — research writing addresses readers who know *less* than you, unlike undergraduate writing for teachers

## Anti-patterns
- **Describing the problem in sentence 1**: you can't state a problem until readers have background; most papers don't begin this way
- **Skipping the tasks**: reading without building the model gives intellectual understanding but not the ability to produce
- **Translation dependence**: relying on translators/proofreaders fails because grammatically-correct-but-wrong-meaning errors are invisible to them

## Worked Example
The book's core method applied to a model Introduction (polylactide/rubber blends) — each numbered sentence is assigned a *function*:
1. "PLA has received much attention…" → establishes importance
2. "PLA is a polymer obtained from corn…" → general background (Present Simple = accepted fact)
3. "It has many possible uses in the biomedical field…" → more specific background + references
4. "However, it has been found to be too weak…" → general problem area
5. "One way to toughen polymers is…" → transition to literature review
6. "Penney et al. showed that…" → overview of key research
7. "However, … little attention has been paid to…" → gap in research
8. "The present paper presents a set of criteria…" → describes the paper
9. "On the basis of these criteria it then describes…" → methodology outline
10. "This combination… formed a novel copolymer…" → announces findings

## Key Takeaways
1. Collect target articles before writing — they are your field-specific template
2. Always answer the Three Questions per section before drafting
3. Write the report sections first, Abstract last
4. Use the model to check drafts: is each component present and in order?
5. Ask a native speaker to check your final text
