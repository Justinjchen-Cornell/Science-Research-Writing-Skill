# Chapter 4: Unit 3 — Writing about Results

## Core Idea
**Results do not speak for themselves.** Graphs and tables show the data; the Results section communicates your *interpretation* of it — what's significant, how it answers the aim, how it compares. If you don't comment on your results in words, readers may read them differently from you, and your conclusions will seem arbitrary.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The 4-Component Results Model (menu)**:
  1. **Revisit the research aim / existing research** → revisit/expand methodology → general overview of results
  2. **Invitation to view results** ("As can be seen in Fig. 1…") → specific/key results in detail (with or without explanations) → comparisons with other research → comparisons with model predictions
  3. **Problems with results** (mention + minimise)
  4. **Possible implications of results**
- **Four Result-Writing Language Systems**:
  1. **Sequence** (8 groups): beforehand → at the beginning → then/next (order only) → soon (short gap) → later (long gap) → when/simultaneously (same time) → at the end → afterwards
  2. **Frequency** (11 groups, 100%→0): always/invariably → generally/usually → regularly → often/frequently → more often than not → as often as not (neutral 50%) → sometimes → occasionally → rarely/seldom → hardly ever → never. *Subjective use: 18% can be "frequent" if previous research predicted ~0%*
  3. **Quantity** (5 groups): make it look big (a considerable amount, as many as 45, substantial) / small (barely, few, as few as 45, negligible) / emphasise (even higher, exceptionally low, by far) / similar-close (almost, nearly, close to) / uncommitted (some, moderate, fairly, to some extent)
  4. **Causality** (with risk control): strong (cause, produce, due to, result in/from — *note "x results from y" = y is the cause; "result in" = y is the effect*) → partial (be a factor in, contribute to) → initial (originate in, initiate) → weak (*be related to, link, associate* — marked * in the book's list)
- **Softening a causal claim** (hedging ladder): weaken the verb (caused → was linked to) → begin with "It appears that / The evidence suggests that / There is a clear possibility that" → add frequency qualifier (x often caused y) → add quantity qualifier (in many cases) → add modal (x may have caused y). Too many → the sentence means nothing

## Key Concepts
- **"The effect occurred in 23% of cases" is a wasted sentence** — the reader can see 23%; say "in as many as 23%" (strong) or "in only 23%" (weak)
- **Quantity language replaces or comments on numbers**: "many" replaces; "as many as 45" comments
- **Section titles vary**: Results / Analysis / Data Analysis / Results and Discussion — check the Guide for Authors

## Mental Models
- "Words have surprising power" — the same curve can look *similar* or *noticeably different* depending on your comment; you control the reader's perception
- "The reader must be able to repeat and compare" — sequence and frequency precision is what makes results reproducible and evaluable

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting tables/graphs carry the section**: you lose the chance to frame interpretation
- **Unhedged causal claims**: "x caused y" can be disproved; use the softening ladder
- **Ambiguous sequence words**: then/next give order but not timing — specify duration and gaps
- **Frequency terms used without care**: always/never are strong claims — reserve for every-occasion results

## Worked Example
Model Results (CO exposure during peak hours) maps to the components:
- S1-S2 → Component 1: revisit existing research (fixed monitor data, Figo et al.)
- S3-S5 → Component 1-2: methodology revisit + invitation ("Figure 1 shows the results…")
- S6-S8 → Component 2: key results + comparison with previous studies, with *However* flagging the anomaly
- S9-S10 → Component 2: specific results detailed, using quantity/frequency language ("considerably lower", "frequently far lower")
- S11-S12 → Component 3: analysis method + problem minimised ("slightly exceeds the 50% acceptability limit")
- S13 → Component 4: implications ("may provide more sensitive information…")

## Key Takeaways
1. Comment on every figure's meaning — never just "As can be seen in Fig. 1"
2. Use the 8-group sequence language to make timing precise
3. Use the 11-group frequency scale (know where 50% sits: as often as not)
4. Use the 5-group quantity language to frame numbers as strong or weak
5. Hedge causal claims to survive later disproof
