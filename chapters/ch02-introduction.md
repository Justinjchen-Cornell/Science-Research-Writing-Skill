# Chapter 2: Unit 1 — How to Write an Introduction

## Core Idea
The Introduction is a funnel: start general (why the field matters), narrow to a research gap, then describe your paper. It has **four model components**, and almost every Introduction ends with component 4 (describing the present paper).

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The 4-Component Introduction Model**:
  1. **Establish the importance of your field** → provide background facts (possibly from research) → define terminology in the title → present the problem area/current research focus
  2. **Previous and/or current research and contributions** (the short literature review)
  3. **Locate a gap in the research** → describe the problem you will address → present a prediction to be tested (start with *However/Although*; rare to use a question)
  4. **Describe the present paper** (purpose, strategy, structure)
- **Tense Pairs** (the engine of meaning):
  - Present Simple = permanent situations, accepted facts/truths (your decision whether findings qualify)
  - Present Continuous = temporary situations (rare in science writing)
  - Past Simple = findings tied to your study only; past research events ("Penney et al. showed…")
  - Present Perfect = findings still relevant NOW, or past research whose gap you're now filling ("little attention *has been paid*…")
  - **Rule: tense changes are always meaningful — they signal a change in the information's function**
- **Signalling Language** (sentence connectors by function):
  - Cause: due to / as / because / since / in view of
  - Result: therefore / as a result / consequently / hence / which is why
  - Contrast: however / on the other hand / whereas / while / but / by contrast (*conversely/on the contrary* = exact opposite, not mere difference)
  - Unexpectedness: although / despite / nevertheless / nonetheless / even so
  - Addition: in addition / moreover / furthermore / what is more
- **Sentence Connection (4 ways)**: overlap (repeat a concept), pro-forms (it/this method/these systems), semicolons/relative clauses, signalling connectors

## Key Concepts
- **Meeting-place fact**: start with the most general fact everyone shares, then narrow
- **Literature review ordering**: chronological / by approach-theory / general-to-specific (avoid the "tennis match" effect of alternating However…On the other hand)
- **Dummy subject**: "This paper presents…" / "The present paper describes…" — clearer than agentless passive ("x is presented")
- **we** = research group only; never "we" for humanity (use "It is known that…")

## Mental Models
- "The space between a full stop and the next capital letter is a dangerous space" — always signal the link between sentences
- "Don't be shy about stating why your field is important" — if you don't establish significance, it looks like your research is NOT significant
- "Reference = research map" — the names you cite draw a map of the field and position your work in it

## Anti-patterns
- **One-sentence paragraphs / overlong paragraphs**: both confuse readers; plan each paragraph around a topic sentence
- **Gap expressed as a question**: unusual in professional writing; state it as a prediction/hypothesis
- **Random tense changes**: "little attention *was* paid" (Past Simple) means attention may have been paid since; the Present Perfect marks the ongoing gap
- **"Don't start sentences with *so*"** for result (too informal); avoid *while/since* ambiguity

## Worked Example
The book's model Introduction ("The synthesis of flexible polymer blends from polylactide and rubber") maps sentence-by-sentence to the 4 components:
- S1-S2 → Component 1 (importance + background, Present Simple for facts)
- S3 → Component 1, more specific, with citations
- S4 → problem area (brief! don't detail your own work yet)
- S5-S6 → Component 2 (transition sentence, then overview of key studies)
- S7 → Component 3 (gap, signalled by *However*)
- S8-S10 → Component 4 (present paper → methodology preview → findings announcement, linked by pro-forms "On the basis of these criteria…" / "This combination…")

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with significance + background facts, not your problem
2. Order the lit review logically (chronological / by approach / general-to-specific)
3. Signal the gap with However/Although before describing your paper
4. Use Present Perfect for research whose gap persists NOW
5. Describe the paper with dummy subjects ("The present paper…"), Present Simple for the paper itself, Past Simple for the aim that preceded the work
