# Patterns — Techniques & Models

## Introduction Model (4 components, must-use order)
**When to use**: writing any research article or thesis Introduction
**How**: (1) establish importance + background facts + define title terms + present problem area; (2) overview previous/current research; (3) locate a gap (However/Although); (4) describe the present paper
**Trade-offs**: following the model makes errors less visible; deviating confuses readers about your paper's purpose

## Methodology Model (4 components, menu-style)
**When to use**: writing Materials and Methods / Procedure / Experimental / Simulation
**How**: (1) general overview + restate purpose + source of materials + essential background; (2) precise details + justify choices + show care; (3) relate to other studies; (4) indicate problems (if any)
**Trade-offs**: include only what applies — a menu, not a checklist; full detail is required for replication but don't pad

## Results Model (4 components, menu-style)
**When to use**: writing Results / Analysis / Data Analysis
**How**: (1) revisit aim/existing research/methodology + overview results; (2) invite viewing + detail key results + compare with other research/model predictions; (3) problems with results; (4) implications
**Trade-offs**: comment on results' meaning — "the effect occurred in 23% of cases" alone is a wasted sentence

## Discussion Model (4 components, reverse-Introduction)
**When to use**: writing Discussion / Results and Discussion / Conclusions
**How**: (1) revisit previous research, methodology, results; (2) map to existing research; (3) achievement/contribution + implications + applications; (4) limitations + future work
**Trade-offs**: mention limitations only as doors for future research; claim novelty only with "to our knowledge"

## Abstract Models (2 options)
**When to use**: writing the Abstract (last!)
**How**: Model 1 = structured, components separate in order (broad topics); Model 2 = flexible, 2-3 components combined, methodology before results (narrow topics)
**Trade-offs**: Model 1 is safer for general audiences; Model 2 is more common and compact — journal decides

## Tense Pair System
**When to use**: choosing verb tense in any section
**How**: Present Simple = accepted facts (your choice whether findings qualify); Present Continuous = temporary; Past Simple = your/others' completed research; Present Perfect = research still relevant NOW; passive Present = standard procedure; passive Past = your own steps
**Trade-offs**: tense changes always signal function changes — random changes corrupt meaning invisibly

## Hedging Ladder (causal softening)
**When to use**: any causal claim you can't fully prove (Results/Discussion)
**How**: weaken the verb (caused → was linked to) → "It appears that… / The evidence suggests that…" → frequency qualifier (often) → quantity qualifier (in many cases) → modal (may have caused). Escalate only as needed
**Trade-offs**: unhedged claims get disproved; over-hedged sentences mean nothing

## Modal Verb Calibration
**When to use**: signalling certainty in Discussion/Abstract
**How**: can = ability; may/might/could = possibility; should/ought to = probable OR advisable (know which!); must/have to = virtually certain (no proof) OR necessary
**Trade-offs**: "must" communicates absence of evidence when used for certainty; cannot ≠ can not (impossible ≠ possibly not)

## Sentence Connection Techniques
**When to use**: every transition between sentences
**How**: overlap (repeat a concept) / pro-forms (it, these systems) / semicolon or relative clause / signalling connector
**Trade-offs**: unconnected sentences force the reader to guess the relationship — the most common error in science writing

## Target Article Protocol
**When to use**: starting any new writing project
**How**: collect 3-4 papers (English-speaking institution, <15 pages, IMRD-subtitled, topic-close); mark what each sentence does; build your model; test it on more papers; write
**Trade-offs**: book chapters and non-IMRD articles are invalid targets — wrong structure to learn from

## Skim-Before-Read (Helicopter Method)
**When to use**: reading any long scientific text efficiently
**How**: read title → author → date → abstract → first paragraph → first sentence of each paragraph → figures/tables titles → last paragraph (especially Summary/Conclusion)
**Trade-offs**: 8 quick steps (minutes, not reading); it's pre-reading, not a substitute

## Problem-Minimising Vocabulary
**When to use**: mentioning problems/limitations anywhere (esp. Methodology, Discussion)
**How**: pair the negative with a positive in the same sentence; use minimising language ("variation among samples was negligible"; "slightly exceeds the limit")
**Trade-offs**: honesty required — minimise framing, never hide the problem
